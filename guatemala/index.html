Destination Guide

Guatemala Travel Guide

A week covers the essentials, ten to fourteen days lets you slow down properly. Most trips start in Antigua for two or three days, colonial streets, chocolate and coffee workshops, before climbing Acatenango for an overnight next to an actively erupting neighbour. From there it's Lake Atitlán's handful of very different lakeside towns, with Semuc Champey's turquoise pools and the Mayan ruins at Tikal as worthwhile extensions if the itinerary stretches to two weeks.

Quick Facts

Suggested trip length

7, 10 or 14 days

Main regions covered

Antigua, Acatenango, Lake Atitlán (San Pedro, San Juan), Semuc Champey, Tikal

Currency

Guatemalan quetzal

Best time for Acatenango

Dry season for clearer skies (colder nights); wet season is warmer but risks rain and fog

Common Questions

How many days do I need in Guatemala?

A week covers the core route: Antigua, Acatenango and Lake Atitlán. Ten to fourteen days lets you add Semuc Champey and Tikal without rushing. Two days in Antigua alone is enough for the markets, churches and a chocolate making workshop.

Is climbing Acatenango hard?

It's split into two real climbs: a 4 to 7 hour slog to base camp on day one depending on fitness, then an entirely dark, pre-dawn summit push on day two. Altitude sickness is a real risk since Antigua itself sits around 1,500 metres, so a couple of acclimatising days there first helps.

Do I need to carry my own gear up Acatenango?

No. If hauling 10 to 15 kilos up a mountain sounds miserable, some companies will carry your bag up for you, and it's money well spent. Base camp comfort varies a lot depending on which company you book with.

What's the best time to climb Acatenango?

Dry season gets you colder nights but clearer skies for watching Fuego erupt opposite your camp. Wet season is a bit warmer but comes with a real risk of rain and fog blocking the view entirely.

Why is Lake Atitlán described as five different towns rather than one place?

Each town around the lake has a genuinely different personality: San Pedro is the backpacker anchor, San Juan leans into Mayan crafts and a stingless bee sanctuary, and others range from hippy to horse-riding to sunrise-hike focused. Basing yourself in just one misses most of what the lake actually offers.

What's Semuc Champey?

A limestone bridge hiding a series of turquoise pools in the jungle near Lanquín, sacred to the Maya. Getting there usually involves standing on the back of a moving jeep, and tubing down the Cahabón River is a common add-on.

Is Tikal worth the detour?

Tikal is one of the greatest Mayan cities, built around 600 BC and once home to nearly 100,000 people, with roughly 85% of it still buried underground. It's a genuine detour north, but the scale of it is hard to find anywhere else in Guatemala.
