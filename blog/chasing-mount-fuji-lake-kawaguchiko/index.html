24 Hours Chasing Mount Fuji at Lake Kawaguchiko: A Ryokan, an Onsen and One Stubborn Cloud

When you imagine a trip to Japan, this is the bit you imagine. A traditional ryokan, paper doors, and Mount Fuji framed in the window. We gave ourselves 24 hours at Lake Kawaguchiko to make it happen. Fuji had other ideas.

Kiran Morjaria

25 July 2026

After five days in Tokyo where we'd eaten everything and seen everything, we headed for Lake Kawaguchiko, at the base of Japan's most iconic mountain. The bucket list here was a short one: see Fuji, sleep in a ryokan, soak in an onsen, and eat the famous noodles. Simple. What we didn't plan for was spending most of our time staring at a very large, very stubborn cloud, because it turns out Fuji is a lot harder to actually see than it looks in everyone's photos.

We started at Tokyo Station, wrestling the Shinkansen. The bullet train there is so long you have to walk fifteen carriages down just to reach carriage fifteen, and Tokyo Station has ready made bento boxes lined up for travellers, which only really happens in Japan. We took it to Mishima rather than going straight to the lake, partly because we were carrying on to Kyoto afterwards and didn't want to backtrack all the way to Tokyo, and partly because there's a lot around the lake you need a car for. It's clean, quiet, and flying along at over 250km/h you're somewhere else before you know it. We even caught a quick glimpse of Fuji from the window, which felt special at the time, although we didn't yet know it might be the only Fuji we'd get all day.

At Mishima we picked up a little Toyota, grabbed the obligatory 7-Eleven smoothie, and set off for Kawaguchiko. It didn't go smoothly. We ended up in some kind of special fast pass lane at the tolls that we couldn't get through, so they had to close the lane behind us and send a man out to rescue us. Lesson learned: when you pick up the car, set it to the no toll route, which is both more scenic and free. Once we were clear of that, though, driving in Japan is genuinely the easiest I've done anywhere. Everyone follows the rules and the roads are big and open.

Easiest country I've ever driven in, once we escaped the toll booth.

We'd chosen to stay on the north shore, on the theory that we'd get Fuji and the lake in the same frame from our room. First stop was coffee and a Basque cheesecake at Troisième Marche, because Japan does Basque cheesecake far too well, and then my first proper curry of the trip at a little veggie spot someone in Tokyo had put me onto, sat on bamboo mats on the floor eating veggie katsu and some wobbling tofu I ordered purely off a photo in the menu. Outside, Fuji sat completely shrouded in cloud. It's a sacred mountain in Shinto, guarded by the goddess Konohanasakuya-hime, the spirit of the cherry blossoms, who once walked into a burning house to prove her loyalty and gave birth unharmed in the flames. Some say those clouds around the summit are hers and they never really left. Standing there looking at a wall of grey, I could believe it.

The ryokan itself was everything you want one to be. Tatami floors, futon beds you make up yourself out of a cupboard, and robes to wear down to dinner. Before that we went to soak at Fuji Yurari Onsen. Onsen are Japan's natural hot springs, and there are thousands of them here thanks to all the volcanic activity, Fuji included. You go in nude, it's separated by gender, and there are no cameras for obvious reasons, so you'll have to take my word for it. It was one of the best I've been to, indoor and outdoor pools, and there was this moment watching four naked old Japanese men quietly staring at half of Mount Fuji appearing as the sun set. Back home in the UK you'd pay ten times as much for something a fraction as good. It's much more of an old school public bath than a spa, and there's something oddly freeing about being nude with fifty strangers.

Dinner back at the ryokan was a full traditional spread, cooked partly at the table. Teppanyaki beef, tempura shrimp, sashimi with tuna and scallops, a pickled octopus salad, and a cabbage dumpling, all of it fresh and beautifully laid out. Then it was an early night, because the whole point of the next morning was to be up before sunrise.

Dinner at the ryokan. Cooked half at the table, all of it fresh.

5:30am, still cloudy, and we set off for the most famous Fuji viewpoint of them all, Chureito Pagoda. On the drive there, out of nowhere, the mountain suddenly appeared through the clouds glowing orange like something from another planet. It didn't look real, more like a painting, and then just as quickly it was gone again. At the pagoda there's a sign warning of a bear in the area, singular, as if they know exactly which one is bothering people. Even at 6:30 in the morning there were already crowds, so we climbed the 400 steps and waited. And waited. Hours later the summit was still buried in cloud, so we gave up and started back down. Of course, the second you stop looking, that's when it decides to show itself.

Chureito at 6:30am. Quiet, cold, and the mountain still hiding.

Our route home took us past a Lawson convenience store with Fuji looming behind it, one of those postcard spots people fly across the world to photograph. It sounds strange to spend a morning driving around looking for different places to admire the same mountain, but when you're actually stood in front of it you can't stop. Back at the ryokan my triumphant post hunt breakfast was a single piece of broccoli and one egg, but honestly the best part was the view from our floor bed. A window framing Fuji perfectly, the exact shot you picture before you ever come to Japan, and somehow even better than we'd imagined.

The view from the floor bed. This is the whole reason you come.

The rest of the day was spent walking the lakeside, which on the north shore is peak aesthetic Japan, markets either side and everyone gathered to photograph the view we happened to have from our window. We'd earned a matcha by this point, so we had a sakura matcha with Fuji in the window at Brand New Day Coffee, which might be the most Japanese thing I did all trip. There's a flower garden nearby too, absolutely heaving with people but for good reason, rows of colour with the mountain behind. My favourite frame of the whole morning, though, was Fuji through a tangle of cherry blossom just starting to bud.

Fuji through the first of the blossom. Worth the wait on its own.

Later we drove up a set of windy roads to Tenku no Torii, a small worship site on a hill with red torii gates and Fuji sitting right behind them. You can feel why people treat this mountain as spiritual once you're stood up there. Then we went full circle and headed back up to Chureito while Fuji was finally out. It was night and day compared to the morning: where there'd been five or six of us at dawn there were now literally hundreds, giant tour buses unloading day trippers and the streets packed. But there she was, and just before the clouds rolled back in we got her with the pagoda in front.

Tenku no Torii. Fuji peeping through the gates.

The local marketing strategy is essentially to put Fuji in front of anything and sell it, so there are Fuji apples the size of my head, Fuji sweets and Fuji mochi, and I respect the hustle. To finish we went for hoto, which is Yamanashi's soul food, wide flat noodles in a miso broth that's basically a hearty mountain stew, specific to this Fuji region. We had ours at Kanjukuya and it came out as a giant bubbling pot, the noodles soft and the broth exactly what you want after a cold morning outdoors. If you want the wider rundown of every spot to actually see the mountain from, I put together a separate guide to the best Fuji viewpoints around the lake.

Hoto noodles at Kanjukuya. Yamanashi's soul food.

The thing about Fuji is it doesn't care about your itinerary, or golden hour, or the fact you've climbed 400 steps before breakfast. It comes when it wants to, and standing there waiting for it you understand exactly why people have been worshipping this mountain for thousands of years. We came expecting a postcard and got something you can't really plan for, a mountain that made us wait but was completely worth it.
