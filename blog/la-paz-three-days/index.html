Death Road, the Witches' Market and a Story I Can't Shake: Three Days in La Paz

The short version

  • La Paz sits in a giant mountain bowl nearly 4,000 metres up, and you feel the altitude the moment you arrive.
  • I started by cycling Death Road, a narrow track cut into a cliff where two to three hundred people a year used to die.
  • The Witches' Market is rows of shops selling offerings to Pachamama, Mother Earth, including the dried llama fetuses families bury under new buildings for good luck.
  • The city runs on cable cars, Mi Teleferico, gliding over the rooftops like ski lifts instead of buses stuck in the hills.
  • A walking tour turned into the most unsettling thing I've learned travelling South America, hearing how far the offerings to Pachamama are said to go.

La Paz might be the most underrated city in South America. It's built inside a mountain bowl nearly 4,000 metres up, the public transport flies over your head, people chew coca leaves on every corner, and by the end of three days it had given me both the best adrenaline rush of the trip and a story I still can't quite shake.

Kiran Morjaria

15 September 2026

I decided to start our time in La Paz by cycling down one of the most dangerous roads in the world. If you're wondering why anyone would willingly travel down a road literally called Death Road, the honest answer is Top Gear. There's a Bolivia special where they drive it, and it's one of the most epic things I've ever seen on telly, so here we were. It's called Death Road because it's a narrow track cut into the side of a mountain with a steep drop straight off one edge, two to three hundred people a year used to die on it, and half the surface is so rocky that you get forced over towards the cliff side whether you like it or not. At points I was maybe 20 or 30 centimetres from the edge, holding my breath, with the whole thing shrouded in cloud. I was, to put it politely, pooping my pants.

A reminder, halfway down, of exactly why they call it Death Road.

What surprised me was that it turned out to be about so much more than not falling off a cliff. The road runs through a community that was let down for years by a government that couldn't fix a road that was killing people, so people took it into their own hands, some of them working unpaid as human traffic lights to stop the accidents. You ride under waterfalls, past wrecks rusting in the ravine below, and past families still scratching a living out here. Both the adrenaline and the people made it a genuine ten out of ten. And somehow, I made it back in one piece.

La Paz itself hits you straight away, mostly because of the altitude. The city sits in a bowl nearly 4,000 metres up, often called the highest capital in the world, and you feel breathless just walking up a street. It's a happening, bustling place though, full of cool little eateries and shops, and everyone we met was incredibly friendly. Two things you notice immediately: everyone is chewing coca leaves, which Andean people have used for thousands of years to cope with altitude, hunger and fatigue, and the city is absolutely full of custom Patagonia gear. Patagonia the brand is from Chile, not Bolivia, but the "Patagonia" fleeces here are everywhere and, questionable authenticity aside, honestly look brilliant.

We went to the Coca Museum to understand the leaf a bit better, and it's genuinely fascinating. It walks you through how important coca is in indigenous culture, with evidence of it being used here for around 8,000 years, and then the darker side, how the leaf was later processed into cocaine. They're two very different things. The museum explains how coca was used in early wines and in Coca-Cola back when the drink still contained cocaine, and how, even after cocaine was banned, coca leaf is apparently still used in the flavouring today. Our guide told us Peru is the only country allowed to legally export coca leaf for things like anaesthetics and that flavouring. I still don't quite know where I sit with all of it, but I loved learning the history of something so central to life here. And then, as true Indians who've been on the road for months and were missing home food, we went for a curry. No notes.

The Coca Museum, which does a good job of separating the leaf from the drug.

The next morning started with a proper full breakfast at Devoto's Tipika, a few minutes from our hostel, Wild Rover, for about $5. An absolute bargain, and the coffee was excellent. With only really 24 hours to do everything we wanted, we threw ourselves into the city. La Paz feels different to anywhere else in South America. Minibuses ferry people around, women walk the streets in polleras and bowler hats, the walls are covered in graffiti and cable cars glide overhead. It was colonised by Spain in the 16th century, sitting on some of the richest silver in the world, but repeated uprisings meant Bolivia held onto much of its indigenous identity. Today somewhere around half to sixty percent of people here identify as Aymara or Quechua, and you feel it everywhere, in the people, the land and the beliefs.

The old-town streets, all cobbles, cables and steep climbs.

One of those beliefs is Pachamama, Mother Earth, and it's the thread running through the Witches' Market, locally the Mercado de las Brujas. It's the most surreal market I've ever walked through, shop after shop of offerings, herbs, incense, little deities, gemstones and, hanging in rows above the stalls, dried llama fetuses. In Andean belief, when you build a house you bury a llama fetus in the foundations as an offering to Pachamama, asking for protection. You give something to the earth, and the earth gives back. A shopkeeper called Blanca saw how curious we were and invited us in to show us how the offerings are actually put together and how the rituals are done, walking us through the sugar, the sweets and the little tokens that each stand for something you're asking for, health, work, money, a home. She even threw in a little llama keychain for me to give my niece, and I came away with the colourful poncho I'd been after. It meant a lot that she took the time, because otherwise you just look and read things online without ever really understanding.

Dried llama fetuses at the Witches' Market, sold as offerings to Pachamama.

Then it was back for the Patagonia fleeces we'd spent the previous night dreaming about. You can customise everything here, the material, the zip, the pockets, even have it made to measure if you've got the time, which we didn't. If you are going custom, we looked at basically every shop and the best one is right next to a cafe called Cafe del Mundo. Jackets secured, we went for lunch at the most popular spot in the city, which happens to be called Popular. It's a place doing proper fine dining for a fraction of the usual price, a three course menu for around $10, and because of that you can't book and the queue is long. We turned up 20 minutes early and only just squeezed in. Starter was a chocolate concoction with fresh cheese, my main was a take on a traditional pumpkin stew heavy with plantain and quinoa, and dessert was a sweet pumpkin empanada with passion fruit. It was one of the best meals we've had in South America, all local ingredients, beautifully done. Bougie backpacking at its finest.

A three course fine-dining lunch at Popular, all in for about $10.

In the afternoon we joined a city walking tour, which is always the best way to actually learn a place from a local. Our guide talked us through the long fight for indigenous rights, and pointed out things we'd walked past three times and never noticed, bullet holes on buildings in the main square, and a clock on the congress building that runs backwards to reflect Andean thinking. We stopped at San Pedro Prison, famous around the world as the jail that essentially ran itself. Prisoners used to buy or rent their own cells, so the more money you had the better you lived, and inside there were whole neighbourhoods with shops, food stalls and hairdressers. There was even a time backpackers paid inmates for tours while it was still operating. These days an ex-inmate known as Crazy Dave stands out in the square telling stories of his fourteen years inside, well, kind of inside.

To get up out of the bowl we jumped on the cable car, and it's genuinely one of the best public transport systems I've ever used. The cabins glide over the city like ski lifts, red brick houses stacked on red brick houses all the way up to El Alto on the plateau above. Our guide said the same trip used to take an hour by bus, whereas the first leg of the cable car took about five minutes and cost three bolivianos. Even if you just ride it for the views, it's worth it. Up top, the market streets of El Alto are full of cholitas, the incredibly well dressed women in bowler hats, heavy pollera skirts and bright shawls, who are fiercely proud of being Bolivian. Some show their wealth with gold on their teeth, almost like tribal grills, and around the markets you'll also find amautas, traditional Andean spiritual leaders a bit like shamans, who people visit for help with health, luck, business or relationships.

Riding Mi Teleferico out of the bowl, the whole city stacked up below.

The El Alto markets up on the plateau, more local and less touristy than the centre.

The tour is also where things got a lot darker. Our guide explained the belief behind the offerings we'd seen at the Witches' Market, that Pachamama is said to accept only three things, alcohol, sugar and blood, and that blood only comes into it for something big, when you're taking a lot from the earth and have to give a lot back. That's the reason for the dried baby llamas, which she was keen to stress die of natural causes, because they're seen as too innocent to be killed. But she went further than that, into an unsettling account of how, for the very biggest constructions, the tradition is said to have involved human offerings, and how she believes that in some form it still quietly happens. She talked about it plainly, and asked us not to photograph the actual rituals because they're private and sacred. I'm not going to pretend I can verify any of it, and I've left the heaviest details where she told them, on the tour. But it was honestly one of the more harrowing things I've heard travelling South America, and it gave me a real appreciation for how much I don't understand about cultures I'm only passing through.

La Paz is underrated, and three days barely scratched the surface. It feels like a place you could spend weeks in and still be finding new corners, ideally while wandering those breathless streets in your new questionable Patagonia. If you'd rather the tidied-up, top-things version of the city than our slightly chaotic three days, I put the highlights together in a separate La Paz guide. And just before we got here, we crossed somewhere even more surreal, the Uyuni Salt Flats, which is worth a read too.
