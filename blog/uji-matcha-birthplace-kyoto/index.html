A Day in Uji: The Little Town Near Kyoto Where Matcha Actually Comes From

The short version

  • Uji sits about 20 minutes south of Kyoto and is where matcha in Japan actually comes from.
  • Nakamura Tokichi Honten (founded 1854) runs a ticketed queue. Grab a number the moment they open and order the matcha parfait.
  • Horii Shichimeien grows its own leaf on Okunoyama, the last of Uji's original seven tea gardens, and sells out within about 15 minutes of opening.
  • For matcha lattes, mid grade ceremonial is plenty. You only really taste the expensive stuff when you drink it neat.

Matcha prices are up over 100% in a year, some of Japan's biggest producers are rationing how much you can buy, and every coffee shop back home has it on the menu. So instead of drinking it out of extinction with everyone else, I went to Uji, the place it actually comes from, to buy it at the source.

Matcha is having a proper moment. It's all over TikTok and Instagram, every coffee shop in the UK has it on the menu, and it's caused a genuine global shortage. The good ceremonial grade stuff has become so popular that Japan physically can't grow enough of it, the price has jumped over 100% in the last year, and some of the biggest tea producers are now rationing how much you're even allowed to buy. So while everyone else was drinking it out of extinction, I wanted to head straight to the source. The plan was simple: track down the actual birthplace of matcha, buy it from the people who grow it, and tick off as many iconic matcha experiences as I could in one afternoon.

Twenty minutes south of Kyoto sits Uji, the home of matcha in Japan. I was based in Kyoto anyway for this leg of the trip, the same one where I drove out to the Japanese Grand Prix at Suzuka, and Uji is an easy hop from there. Tea fields cover the hillsides and the streets are lined with historic tea houses, some of which have been running for centuries. You can practically feel the matcha in the air.

Nakamura Tokichi Honten, founded 1854. People fly halfway around the world for this one.

Our first stop was Nakamura Tokichi Honten, founded in 1854 and probably the most famous matcha spot in the whole of Uji. We showed up about ten minutes after they opened and grabbed a ticket, and I was already number 71 in the queue. At least they've got a QR code system, so rather than stand around you can wander off and check on your phone when your number is getting close. Ours was hours away, which turned out to be perfect, because it meant we could go and do everything else first.

While that ticket cooked, we ducked down the road to Horii Shichimeien. This place grows its own leaf on Okunoyama, a 600 year old tea garden that happens to be the last survivor of Uji's original seven gardens. So this is genuine farm to cup matcha rather than a shop reselling someone else's harvest, and it recently went viral for a blend that's supposed to make the best matcha latte in the world. You know I love a matcha latte. The tea here disappears fast though. They only sell a handful of tins per variety each day, and within about 15 minutes of opening most of it is gone.

We came away with two tins. One called Uji Mukashi, which the staff told us is meant to have a really nice umami flavour, and one called Agata no Shiro, which we'd seen all over the internet as the MVP for matcha lattes. The two of them set us back about $35 together, so not the cheapest matcha in the world, but the quality is supposed to be incredible. Handy thing I learned here: matcha should be kept in the fridge, and it stays good for up to six months once it's open.

The streets of Uji are dead quiet, but the tea shops are anything but.

While we walked, here's the quick version of why matcha is so different to normal green tea. Regular green tea is grown in full sun, steeped in hot water, and then the leaves get thrown away. Matcha comes from the same plant, but for the last few weeks before it's picked the bushes are covered up and shaded from the sun. Deprived of light, the plant panics and pumps out huge amounts of chlorophyll and amino acids just to survive. The leaves are then de-stemmed, dried flat, and stone ground into a powder so fine that when you drink it you're consuming the entire leaf. That's why the colour is so electric, the caffeine a touch higher, and the flavour rich and savoury instead of watery.

You can watch the leaves get stone ground into powder. That's the secret to it being silky rather than gritty.

By this point we'd done enough buying, so it was time to eat. We stopped at the Matcha Roastery, a newer spot with a menu that covers pretty much everything you can imagine, drinks, cookies, cakes, ice cream, mochi. We went for a matcha Basque cheesecake and a yuzu soda, and honestly that cheesecake was so dense and umami it was one of the best matcha desserts of the whole day. The more I ate it the more I liked it. It's genuinely unlike anything I've had before.

By the time we walked back, our number was finally up at Nakamura Tokichi. They start you off with a welcome drink of kabosu cha, and then the absolute must order is the matcha parfait, which arrives stacked inside a length of bamboo. Matcha ice cream, whipped cream, lemon jam, jelly, berries, red beans, it all sounds a bit weird written down but it completely works, that mix of umami and slightly sweet that's the way I like matcha anyway. We split it because it's absolutely huge, and had it alongside a matcha float topped with soft serve. The photos don't do the size of these justice. Easily one of the biggest but most photogenic things I ate on the entire trip.

The parfait at Nakamura Tokichi. Sounds strange on paper, works perfectly.

The matcha float with soft serve, next to the Basque cheesecake from earlier.

Back out on the streets, it's shop after shop, each vendor doing matcha slightly differently. You can just walk around, watch how a place makes it, try some, buy a thing, eat it, and move on to the next. It is genuinely so much fun, truly matcha heaven. A couple of things stuck with me. One, there are far more types of matcha than I ever realised. Two, ceremonial grade is obviously the higher quality stuff and it gets seriously expensive, but the price climbs exponentially while the taste barely does. When you're having it as a latte, which is how most of us drink it at home, mid grade ceremonial is plenty. You honestly can't tell the difference between that and the really pricey stuff once it's in milk. Where you can taste it is when you drink it neat, in water, where the expensive matchas are much less bitter and smell noticeably sweeter.

Iced matcha for a couple of hundred yen a cup. Every shop does it slightly differently.

The other thing you notice is that each place really does cap how much it sells per day, so the scarcity we're feeling at home isn't just clever marketing. They are actually running out. Wandering between shops we kept seeing everyone sell these matcha dango on a stick, so we figured we should give one a try. We ended up regretting that decision, but that's part of the fun of a place like this.

The dango on a stick. Looked great, less of a winner than the parfait.

Our final stop was a modern cafe called Masuda, famous for a soy matcha latte. It tastes like the version we get at home, just turned up several notches. It's the most matchary matcha I've ever had, no bitterness, and none of that graininess you usually get. I reckon that's the stone grinding again, making it silky smooth. By this point we were in matcha heaven but also completely matcha'd out, and I think we'd well and truly done Uji, the birthplace of matcha, with tins to take home and a parfait I'll be dreaming about for a while. If you end up in Japan and you've got an afternoon spare near Kyoto, this one's worth the trip. Full video below.
