Strangers on a Bus and Four Wild Days on Peru's Santa Cruz Trek

The short version

  • The Santa Cruz Trek is a four day hike through the Cordillera Blanca, the highest tropical mountain range in the world, out of Huaraz in northern Peru.
  • It's wild camping the whole way, no huts and no toilets, with donkeys carrying the heavy bags between camps.
  • The high point is Punta Union pass at 4,750m; a worthwhile side trip climbs to Laguna Arhuaycocha, a turquoise glacial lake at 4,420m.
  • The weather turns fast. We got rain, hail and snow, and barely saw the biggest peaks, but the trek was still unforgettable.

Everyone comes to Peru for Machu Picchu. Hidden in the remote mountains of the north is a trek plenty of hikers rate even higher, and almost nobody talks about. Four days through the Cordillera Blanca with seven people I'd met an hour earlier.

Kiran Morjaria

31 August 2026

North Peru is a long way off the usual gringo trail. Most people fly into Lima, head straight for Cusco and Machu Picchu, and never make it up to Huaraz, the scruffy little mountain city that the Santa Cruz Trek runs out of. Which is a shame, because the range it sits under, the Cordillera Blanca, is the highest tropical mountain range in the world, all glaciers, turquoise lakes and huge dramatic peaks. Over four days you walk right through the middle of it. That was the plan, anyway.

It started at 5am in the dark. When a Peruvian tour tells you 5am, it usually means 5:20, so of course this was the one time they turned up dead on 5 and I was nowhere near ready. Our hostel had also had no water the night before or that morning, so I hadn't managed a shower either, which felt like a fitting start. I slept through most of the bumpy bus ride, apart from the parts where my head kept getting bounced off the window, and eventually we all sat down for breakfast somewhere with no real idea what was going on. Someone said fill a duffel and the donkey carries it. Half the group had been told nothing at all. That was pretty much the vibe for the next four days.

The sign at the start. From here it's four days on foot to the other side of the range.

The strange thing about a trek like this is that you meet everyone on the bus an hour before you start, and then you're stuck with them for four days. There were eight of us. We set off up a massive valley with a river running through it and giant cliffs on either side, the light doing something ridiculous through the clouds, and by the time we stopped for our first snacks it already felt like one of the more beautiful walks I'd done. We reached the first camp in the afternoon, said hello to the donkeys, and pitched up.

There are no organised campsites on the Santa Cruz Trek, no mountain huts, and no toilets. Just open valleys where you pitch a tent wherever the ground is flat enough, and pure nature for everything else. We got the tents up just as the rain started and all eight of us crammed into a food tent barely big enough for half that, which turned into an evening of card games and food and more card games while the clouds rolled in and the mountains disappeared into the dark. I was sharing a tent with David, so we got to know each other very quickly. Mat, sleeping bag, liner, thermals, no pillow. It did the job.

Camp on night one. No huts, no toilets, just flat ground and whatever the weather decides to do.

Breakfast the next morning was dry bread, jam, a bit of guacamole and a filter coffee, which is about as five star as it gets this deep into the mountains, and honestly it was lovely. We packed up and before long the valley opened out into the vast Santa Cruz Valley proper, ringed by the towering peaks of the Cordillera Blanca, and for once at 7am the weather was actually perfect. We reached Laguna Jatuncocha, a long glacial lake sitting at 3,800m in the middle of the valley, and pushed on.

The Santa Cruz Valley opening out. A river winding through the middle and walls of rock either side.

This was the longest day, somewhere around 20-something kilometres, and we were the only ones walking in this direction. Everyone we passed was coming the other way, telling us how nice and flat and downhill it had all been, which of course meant that for us it was going to be flat and uphill. Doing it the harder way felt worth it though, walking straight into the mountains rather than away from them. Somewhere around lunchtime a dog appeared and simply decided it was coming with us. It slept at camp and carried on the next morning, and it turned out to be one of the best hikers in the group.

Late in the day we peeled off on a side trip up to Laguna Arhuaycocha. Only six of us fancied the extra climb. It winds up through what genuinely feels like a magical mossy forest, and as the fog lifted, huge snow covered peaks started appearing behind the clouds like a painting slowly coming to life. We were fairly sure the lake was just around the corner several times before it actually was, but when we turned the final bend there it was, a brilliant turquoise lake at 4,420m sitting right beneath the glaciers. I'd done Laguna 69 near Huaraz earlier in the trip, which is stunning but heaving with people. Here it was just the six of us, and the fact we'd had to walk so far to reach it made it hit completely differently. Then the clouds rolled back in, the rain started, and we legged it back down to camp before it got us.

Laguna Arhuaycocha at 4,420m. Worth every extra step, and completely empty.

Back at camp there was a short window of sun, so three of us decided the moment had come to jump in the stream and have a wash. It was, for the record, extremely cold. Refreshing is one word for it. The evening was more card games as the group slowly turned into actual friends, then dinner by candlelight, pasta soup, rice, veg and eggs, which felt weirdly romantic given the setting.

The dog that adopted us at lunchtime and never left. Better on the wet rock than any of us.

Day three started cold and sleepless. It had rained hard all night, dogs barking, David needing to pee in the dark which sounded like an ordeal, and to top it off a cow had wandered off with his towel that he'd hung up to dry. The boys went to face off with the cow to try and get it back, still unsure to this day whether it had eaten the towel or just dropped it somewhere. Scrambled eggs and broccoli, a hot cocoa, and we hit the trail into a whiteout. Bitterly cold, raining, visibility close to zero, thick cloud swallowing every mountain around us. As one of the group from the Highlands of Scotland pointed out, a bit of weather wasn't going to stop us.

We traversed a mountainside at 4,500m, where you really start to feel the altitude, everyone a lot more breathless than the day before, and then climbed for Punta Union, the highest point of the whole trek. The rain slowly turned to snow as we trudged up through the fog, glacial lakes appearing and vanishing in the cloud. We made it to the top at 4,750m. Still snowing, still freezing, still couldn't see a thing, and somehow still brilliant. There was enough snow up there that our guide Samuel had time to build a full snowman before it got too cold to hang around.

The top of Punta Union at 4,750m. Snowing, freezing, zero view, and I loved it anyway.

The way down the other side was genuinely treacherous. No proper steps, just big slabs of rock that would be grippy and fine when dry, but were sheets of ice with the snow and rain coming down. Going down turned out to be far harder than going up, and my knees were not happy about it. Then the landscape flipped completely as we dropped into the Huaripampa Valley, all alpine meadows and waterfalls pouring off the cliffs behind us. We also spent a solid ten minutes of that descent falling over. Someone went down in the mud, then someone else, then a hand landed squarely in a fresh cow pat. By my count we hit the deck about five times between us. At a certain point when it's raining that hard you just have to accept it and enjoy being covered in mud.

Down in the Huaripampa Valley, llamas grazing in the meadows. A good spot to be a llama.

Our last camp was the reward for all of it, a well earned beer sat outside the tents while the mules grazed quietly beside us and the mountains slipped away into the dark one more time. The final morning was pancakes and a much lower energy version of the group than day one. We had a three hour walk back to Vaquería for the bus, which we were somehow told we'd be doing in two.

Four days earlier we'd been strangers on a bus heading into the Andes, and now it felt like we'd been through something properly together. This was not the trek I signed up for. It was wetter, colder and far wilder than the brochure version, and we missed a lot of the white mountains behind the cloud. But adversity has a way of bonding a group of people, and I came away with some genuinely good friends. Maybe the real Santa Cruz Trek is the friends we made along the way. Or maybe it's just the mountains. Probably a bit of both.
