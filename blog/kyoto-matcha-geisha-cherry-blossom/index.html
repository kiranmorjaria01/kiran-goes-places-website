Kyoto in Full Bloom: Matcha, Geisha and Five Days in Old Japan

The short version

  • Uji, the actual home of matcha, is about 20 minutes south of Kyoto by train. Get to Nakamura Tokichi Honten close to its 10am opening or the queue runs into hours.
  • Fushimi Inari's torii gates empty out fast after the Yotsuji intersection halfway up, which is where most people turn around.
  • Gion is best after dark for the wooden streets, the lit-up Yasaka Pagoda and a real chance of spotting a geisha heading to work.
  • Nara and its bowing deer, plus Todai-ji's Great Buddha, make the easiest day trip from the city.

Kyoto was the capital of Japan for over a thousand years, and it still feels like the place the rest of the country keeps its old self. We gave it five days during peak cherry blossom and barely scratched it.

Kyoto had been the part of this Japan trip I was most looking forward to. We'd already been to Tokyo, chased Fuji around Lake Kawaguchiko, and used Kyoto as a base when I drove out to the Japanese Grand Prix at Suzuka. But this was the stretch where we properly slowed down in the old capital. You cannot plan cherry blossoms, you just book your trip and hope, and somehow we landed on full bloom. It meant we'd picked the busiest week of the year, which we found out on the first night when two restaurants turned us away for queues before we found a tiny curry and tempura place that turned out to be one of the best meals of the whole trip.

So the first morning we didn't even stay in Kyoto. We took the train 20 minutes south to Uji, because if you like matcha, this is the source of it. Monks brought powdered tea over from China in the 12th century to stay awake through meditation, and Uji has been the benchmark for Japanese matcha ever since. The whole town is built around it, tea fields up in the hills and tea houses on practically every corner. Our first stop was Nakamura Tokichi Honten, founded in 1854 and the most famous name in Uji, the one everyone flies in for. We got there ten minutes after opening, pulled a ticket, and were already number 71 in the queue. They run it on a QR code so you can wander off and watch your number climb, which we did.

While we waited we went down the road to Horii Shichimeien, a producer with their own tea garden that is centuries old and one of the last of what used to be Uji's seven. This is proper farm to cup matcha, and one of their blends had gone viral as supposedly the best matcha latte in the world. There are only a handful of each type for sale a day, and even fifteen minutes after opening most of it was gone. We walked out with two: a Uji Mukashi for its umami, and the Agata no Shiro that the internet had decided was the MVP for lattes. Useful thing we learned there, matcha is meant to live in the fridge and keeps about six months once it's open. By the time our number finally came up back at Nakamura Tokichi, we sat down to a welcome cup of kabusecha and the matcha parfait everyone comes for, layers of matcha cream, ice cream, jelly, lemon jam and beans in a glass, plus a matcha float served inside a piece of bamboo. It was one of the most delicious things I've eaten, and it was still only 11am.

We kept going, because that is what Uji does to you. A newer spot called Masuda does a soy matcha latte that tastes like the stuff we drink at home but on another planet, no bitterness, no graininess, apparently because it's stone ground. On the edge of town is Byodo-in, and even after a trip full of temples this one stopped us. The Phoenix Hall floats over its own pond and looks exactly like the building on the back of the ten yen coin, because it is the building on the ten yen coin. With the cherry blossoms around the water it looked like a postcard.

Byodo-in's Phoenix Hall in Uji. The same building that's on the ten yen coin.

Back in central Kyoto we headed to Nishiki Market, a 400 year old covered market that still feeds the city. It is not the most vegetarian friendly place in the world, there's wagyu being seared on pretty much every corner, but there's also tempura, sushi, skewers and rice balls, and the very Japanese habit of showing you a plastic model of exactly what you're about to order. We ate rice balls, tempura scallops and some veggie gyoza, and after a morning of nothing but sweet matcha, something salty and crispy was exactly right.

Before heading back we did a slow walk along the Philosopher's Path, a canal lined the whole way with cherry trees. In sakura season the branches hang right over the water and it just goes on and on. These are people's actual houses backing onto it, and I kept thinking about what it must be like to have that outside your front door. It's one of the most beautiful walks I've ever done.

The Philosopher's Path in full bloom. It carries on like this for ages.

The next morning we were up and out by 7am for Fushimi Inari, one of Kyoto's oldest shrines, founded in the year 711 and dedicated to Inari, the god of rice and farming. The legend goes that a nobleman from the Hata clan shot an arrow at a rice cake for target practice, the rice cake turned into a swan and flew off, and where it landed rice started growing, which was taken as a sign. Foxes are considered Inari's messengers, and because they were thought to love the tofu skin, that's where the name Inari sushi comes from. What the shrine is really famous for is the gates, thousands of vermilion torii forming tunnels up the mountain, each one donated by a person or a business as thanks. There are around 1,200 steps to the top. Most people climb for half an hour to the Yotsuji intersection, which looks out over Kyoto, and turn back. If you carry on, or duck right behind a small shrine, the crowds vanish and you get the gates almost to yourself, cherry blossom and little fox shrines and birdsong and nobody else. That's the Fushimi Inari in the photos up top, and it's worth the early start to actually have it quiet.

After breakfast back in Gion we did something I'd really wanted to, a proper tea ceremony. Chado, the way of tea, is a centuries old art form about finding beauty in imperfection and doing things slowly and deliberately. Every ceremony is treated as a thing that will never happen again in quite the same way, so you give it your full attention. We did ours at Maikoya, a traditional wooden townhouse popular enough that we'd booked weeks in advance. You put on split toe socks and a kimono, pick your own style, and sit on little stools with a whisk and a sweet in front of you while the host walks you through it. It genuinely feels like a one person play. They teach you the real way to whisk, fast across the surface first to blend it, then a W or M motion to break the bubbles, then slow circles to pull in the edges, and if you've done it right there's a foam only in the middle. Matcha you've made yourself tastes like nothing else.

The one I whisked myself. Foam in the middle means you got it right.

Gion comes alive after dark. The cherry blossoms get lit up, there's a shrine strung with lanterns, and the cobbled streets feel like stepping back in time. Sannenzaka and Ninenzaka are over a thousand years old and protected so nothing can be modernised, which is how you end up with what has to be the most tasteful Starbucks on earth. There's a legend that if you trip on these streets you'll die within two years, which is a very effective way to stop people falling down a steep hill. The Yasaka Pagoda frames the end of the street, built in the 1400s with no nails, just interlocking wood. We also found Yasui Konpiragu, a shrine for cutting bad ties, its centre buried under thousands of paper wishes you crawl through a hole to leave.

Yasaka Shrine lit up at the top of Gion. The whole area is better at night.

Gion is also where the geisha, or geiko, still live and work. They train for years as masters of dance, music and conversation, live in houses alongside maiko who are geisha in training, and perform in teahouses with very discreet comings and goings. Hanamikoji Street has been the centre of that world since the 17th century, and at the end of it is Ichiriki Ochaya, the most exclusive teahouse in the city, over 300 years old, run by one family, where access comes through relationships passed down the generations. It's tied up with the legend of the 47 ronin, and apparently presidents and the odd Hollywood name have been invited in. Around half ten at night is when the geisha and maiko tend to leave, and after two days of catching glimpses from a distance we got lucky and saw three come out of the teahouse right in front of us. There's a real allure to it, this secret world you're standing next to but will never actually see inside.

Day three we took the train out to Arashiyama for the bamboo forest. It's moso bamboo, one of the fastest growing plants on earth, capable of shooting up over a metre in a single day, and it's one of the most famous spots in the city. Honestly, between the rain, the crowds and a lifetime of seeing it in photos, the grove itself felt a bit underwhelming, and within twenty minutes we'd walked it and looped back.

The Arashiyama bamboo grove. Impressive, but it's over quickly.

What saved Arashiyama was the temple at the entrance, Tenryu-ji, ranked first among Kyoto's five great Zen temples. You can hear the monks chanting, and the garden had some of the most varied flowers we'd seen in Japan. We'd paid to walk the garden when something completely unplanned happened. The temple was running a one off spring event, a tea ceremony inside the main hall served by an actual maiko. After two days trying to see one from across a street, we were suddenly sat right in front of her, being served high grade matcha and watching her move through the whole thing with real elegance. That's what stumbling onto something genuinely rare in travel feels like. Wandering back through the streets we also fell into a chopstick making studio and made our own pair, choosing the wood, shaving it down with a razor sharp hand plane the host wouldn't let us near without a warning, then polishing it with a stick of bamboo and finishing it with camellia oil.

Back towards the centre we wound up through Ninenzaka to Kiyomizu-dera, founded over 1,250 years ago. The stage is one of the great feats of Japanese carpentry, held up by well over a hundred pillars, some of them thirteen metres tall, all locked together with interlocking wooden joints and built straight into the mountainside. It was absolutely pouring, so we missed the sunset, but you could still see the temple on its stilts on one side and the whole city of Kyoto on the other. After days of ramen, sushi and gyoza we then did something completely different and went for pizza, at a place called Pizza Strada we'd heard a New York comedian swear did the best pizza he'd had in Japan. The crust was unlike any I've eaten, and Sam, who will not touch pizza crust at home, ate the whole thing, which tells you everything. We finished the night under the Gion Shidarezakura in Maruyama Park, a giant weeping cherry tree they light up, the most photographed tree in all of Kyoto, and at peak bloom it was honestly a bit haunting.

On the last morning Sam headed off early to Hiroshima, so I started, naturally, with more matcha, a cup with actual gold flakes in it, which felt appropriate because I was on my way to the Golden Temple. Kinkaku-ji is a 14th century temple built as a shogun's show of power, about an hour from the centre, and it is a genuinely incredible structure, the gold properly shining when the sun hits it. The problem was that the sun had hit it, so the place was heaving. It's still worth it, especially sat in its garden with the trees and the lake around it. And it's wild how in Japan you can walk two streets from one of the busiest monuments in the country and suddenly be in a completely dead residential neighbourhood.

Kinkaku-ji, the Golden Temple. Go early, the sun brings the crowds.

There was one place I'd wanted the whole trip and hadn't made it to yet, so I used the last afternoon on Nara. Nara is famous for its deer. The story goes that when Kasuga Taisha shrine was founded in 768 AD, one of the gods turned up riding a white deer, and from then on the deer were treated as sacred messengers. There was even a time when killing one carried the death penalty. Now they roam the park freely, and the reason they're so famous is that they bow. It's a learned behaviour, but if you hold out some food they'll bow their head before they take it, and standing in a field of shrines and sakura with deer bowing at you is about as quintessentially Japanese as it gets. I also had the best coffee of the whole trip here, at a tiny place called Chummy Roastery right by the park.

Nara's deer bow for food. Genuinely one of the strangest, best things to watch.

By this point I'd basically given up on temples, and then I spotted Todai-ji across the park and thought I could just squeeze it in before closing. Walking up to it after expecting nothing was breathtaking. The scale of it is hard to take in, the closest thing I can compare it to is seeing the Taj Mahal. Inside the Great Buddha Hall, one of the oldest wooden buildings in Japan and over a thousand years old, is a giant bronze Buddha flanked by two more huge statues, and it's genuinely difficult to process when you're standing under it. Around the side there's a wooden pillar with a hole carved at its base, exactly the size of one of the Buddha's nostrils, and squeezing through it is supposed to bring good luck. Looking at the size of the hole, I decided that wasn't the kind of luck coming my way today.

The Great Buddha inside Todai-ji. The scale does not come across on camera.

Five days and we still left things on the list. Kyoto does that, it keeps a thousand years of old Japan packed into one city and then throws in a town built entirely around matcha and a park full of bowing deer on its doorstep. If you can time it for the blossoms, do, even if it means the busiest week of the year. Full vlog below.

Video summary

A five day vlog through Kyoto during peak cherry blossom season. It opens with a day trip 20 minutes south to Uji, the home of matcha, queueing at Nakamura Tokichi Honten (founded 1854), buying rare blends at the centuries old Horii Shichimeien tea garden, a matcha parfait and float, a stone ground soy matcha latte at Masuda, and Byodo-in temple, the building on the ten yen coin. Back in Kyoto it covers the 400 year old Nishiki Market and a cherry blossom walk along the Philosopher's Path. Day two is an early climb through the thousands of torii gates at Fushimi Inari (founded 711), a traditional tea ceremony at Maikoya, and Gion after dark: the thousand year old Sannenzaka and Ninenzaka streets, the Yasaka Pagoda, Yasui Konpiragu shrine, and spotting real geisha leaving Ichiriki Ochaya. Day three heads to the Arashiyama bamboo grove and Tenryu-ji, where an unplanned spring event means a tea ceremony served by a maiko, plus a chopstick making workshop, Kiyomizu-dera, pizza at Pizza Strada and the lit up weeping cherry in Maruyama Park. The final day covers the Golden Temple Kinkaku-ji, then Nara, with its bowing sacred deer and the Great Buddha inside Todai-ji.
