Old Japan in One City: The Best Things To Do in Kyoto

The short version

  • Kyoto was Japan's capital for over a thousand years and escaped the wartime bombing, so it still looks like old Japan: wooden streets, geisha districts and temples in every direction.
  • Gion is the geisha quarter. The best chance of seeing a geiko or a maiko is between 5 and 6:30pm on Hanamikoji, and photography is banned on the private side streets, with real fines if you break it.
  • Uji, about 20 minutes south by train, is the birthplace of matcha. Grab a ticket the moment Nakamura Tokichi Honten opens, and buy leaf to take home from Horii Shichimeien.
  • Fushimi Inari's roughly 10,000 red torii gates are best at 6 or 7am before the tour buses arrive. Go right at the halfway point and the crowds almost vanish.
  • Nara, about an hour out, was Japan's first capital, home to the bowing deer and Todai-ji, one of the largest wooden buildings in the world.

Kyoto was the capital of Japan for a thousand years and somehow dodged the war, so it still looks like old Japan: wooden streets, a hidden world of geisha behind closed doors, matcha grown at the source and more temples than you could see in a week. There's genuinely too much to fit into one trip, so here's the list I'd actually plan around.

1. Wander Gion and hope to spot a geisha

Start in Gion, the heart of old Kyoto, and specifically Hanamikoji Street, the centre of the city's geisha world since the 17th century. Geiko, which is the local word for geisha, and their apprentices, the maiko, still live and train here and perform in tea houses most of us will never see the inside of. The most famous is Ichiriki Ochaya, over 300 years old and still run by the same family. It's the kind of place that, apparently, the odd president and the likes of Will Smith and Tom Cruise have been invited into. We got lucky and watched three geiko come out of a tea house right in front of us, and they genuinely take your breath away.

If you want the same chance, the best window is between 5 and 6:30pm, when they leave home for their evening appointments. One heads up though: Kyoto enforces a strict photography ban on the private side streets to protect the geiko and maiko, with heavy fines if you break it, so keep your camera to the main streets like Hanamikoji.

A geiko and a maiko heading to an evening appointment in Gion. Easily the luckiest few seconds of the trip.

2. Take the train to Uji, the home of matcha

From Gion it's about a 20 minute train ride south to Uji, the actual birthplace of matcha in Japan. The whole town is built around tea, with fields on the hills and tea houses on every street, some of them centuries old. We started at Nakamura Tokichi Honten, founded in 1854 and probably the most famous matcha spot in the whole of Uji. Grab a ticket the moment it opens, because we arrived ten minutes after opening and were already number 71. It's famous for the matcha parfait, a mix of matcha jelly, sponge, tiny mochi and ice cream served in a piece of bamboo, and it was genuinely one of the best things I ate on the whole trip.

If you want matcha to take home, I'd buy from Horii Shichimeien, a producer with its own centuries-old tea garden on Okunoyama, the last of Uji's original tea gardens. It was our favourite once we were drinking it back at home. While you're in Uji, a few minutes down the street is Byodo-in, whose Phoenix Hall floats over its pond and looks exactly like the building on the back of the 10 yen coin, because it is the building on the 10 yen coin.

The matcha parfait at Nakamura Tokichi, served in bamboo. Worth the queue ticket.

3. Climb the torii gates at Fushimi Inari

Back nearer central Kyoto is Fushimi Inari, the shrine everyone pictures when they think of the city. It was founded in 711, older than Kyoto itself, and dedicated to Inari, the god of rice and prosperity. Around 10,000 red torii gates form a tunnel from the base of the mountain to the summit, each one donated by a business hoping for good fortune. It's 1,200 steps to the top, but about 30 minutes gets you to the halfway point and a view over the city. A lot of people turn around there, but keep going, and take the right fork rather than the left, and the crowds thin out almost completely. Because the shrine is open 24/7 with no gates, aim to arrive around 6 or 7am to have the glowing red tunnels practically to yourself before the tour buses show up.

4. Do a tea ceremony (and wear a kimono)

So much of Kyoto's history is tied up in the tea ceremony, which is more a ceremonial art form than anything to do with being thirsty, so treat it like watching a performance. We booked ours in the old town and wore kimonos, which I'd recommend on both counts, they make for great photos in the old streets and they're surprisingly comfortable. We watched the host's performance and were taught the actual technique: whisking fast just above the surface in a W motion to break the bubbles, then a few circles to finish. If the foam ends up sitting only in the centre of your whisk, you've done it right.

The thing that stuck with me was something our host said, that when you serve tea to guests you might never be in that exact situation again, never with those people again, never making it quite the same way, so you respect and appreciate every moment. A lovely sentiment that I fully intend to hold onto right up until I'm late for work and rushing my espresso.

In kimono in the old town. Comfier than they look, and made for the streets around here.

5. Eat your way through Nishiki Market

For something a bit busier in central Kyoto, head to Nishiki Market. It's been feeding the city for over 400 years, a long covered street stacked with stalls selling everything from yakitori to okonomiyaki to wagyu beef. We worked through rice balls, tempura scallops and veggie gyoza, all rich and buttery. Handily, most stalls have a model of the food they serve out front, so you know exactly what you're ordering. It's a great spot for a quick lunch on the move.

6. Find the best ramen in Japan at Engine Ramen

If you want to sit down for something more substantial, go to Engine Ramen. It feels a bit odd giving one ramen shop its own spot on a Kyoto list, but this was honestly my favourite ramen in all of Japan. One of the small joys of ramen here is that you also get a bib, a genuinely fetching bib, so when I inevitably splatter the broth everywhere it doesn't wreck my t-shirt. I'm veggie, and their creamy veg ramen with a marinated egg was rich with sesame running through the broth, spicy, with handmade noodles and mushrooms. Unbelievable.

7. Walk the Philosopher's Path

To slow things down, see the prettier side of the city on the Philosopher's Path. It's a 2km canal walk named after a Kyoto University professor who used to meditate here on his daily commute in the early 1900s. In sakura season it's particularly special, the path lined with blossom trees hanging over the water and little cafes tucked along the way. It's the most aesthetic walk I've done, and one of the most peaceful spots in Kyoto.

The Philosopher's Path in full bloom. Two kilometres of this.

8. See the giant cherry tree at Maruyama Park

If you're cherry blossom hunting, make a quick stop at Maruyama Park. It's home to the most photographed cherry tree in Kyoto, the Gion Shidare-zakura, a huge weeping cherry that's lit up after dark. It only takes a few minutes to see, but at peak bloom, lit up at night, it's the single biggest blossom I've ever stood under and easily worth the detour.

9. The Arashiyama bamboo grove and Tenryu-ji

On the western edge of the city is the famous bamboo grove, giant towering stalks that look like another worldly forest. Right beside it is Tenryu-ji, an important Zen temple with a lovely garden near the entrance, and the day we visited they were running a special event where you could have tea with a miko, which was a nice surprise. In all honesty, the bamboo grove itself is quite far out, fairly small and a little underwhelming because of the crowds, but the wider Arashiyama area is worth the trip, especially for what's around the next corner.

10. Make your own chopsticks

Just around the corner from the bamboo grove, you can make your own chopsticks. You choose your wood, shave it down with a traditional plane, sand it and finish it with oil. It's a proper hands-on souvenir and one you'll actually use when you get home, a genuinely fun couple of hours and very reasonable at around $10, chopsticks included.

11. Kinkaku-ji, the golden pavilion

A short trip from the centre and well worth it is Kinkaku-ji, the golden pavilion. It's a 14th century display of pure shogun wealth, the top two floors covered in gold leaf and reflected perfectly in its own pond. It's one of the busiest sites in the city and you move through on a strict one-way route, but the image is unmistakably Kyoto, and sitting in its Japanese garden among the trees and the lake, it's genuinely impressive in real life.

Kinkaku-ji up close. The top two floors are covered in actual gold leaf.

12. Walk Ninenzaka, Sannenzaka and the Yasaka Pagoda

Back in Higashiyama near Gion are two of Kyoto's oldest streets, Ninenzaka and Sannenzaka. Both are over a thousand years old and protected, so nothing here can be modernised, and they're about the most aesthetic old streets I've ever walked. The local legend is that if you trip on these steps you'll die within two years, so I was watching my feet very carefully. Follow them down and you come out near the Yasaka Pagoda, built in the 1400s from interlocking wood with not a single nail in the whole structure. It's lit up at night like something off a postcard, mostly because it's on all the postcards.

13. Kiyomizu-dera and its wooden stage

From there it's a short climb up to Kiyomizu-dera, founded over 1,250 years ago. Its famous wooden stage is held up by more than 100 pillars, some of them 13 metres tall, and the whole thing has survived nearly 400 years of earthquakes. What really gets you when you're standing under it is the sheer scale, this giant temple carried on stilts off the hillside. We'd already seen a lot of temples by this point, but this one is absolutely worth it: unique, impressive and very serene.

Kiyomizu-dera at blue hour, the whole temple held up on wooden stilts.

14. Day trip to Nara for the bowing deer and Todai-ji

For the last stop, take a short ride out to Nara, Japan's first ever capital, to be bowed at by a deer. How Japanese. Legend has it the god of Kasuga Taisha shrine arrived riding a white deer, and ever since the deer here have been treated as sacred messengers, to the point that there was a time when killing one carried the death penalty. What makes them famous now is that they bow. It's a learned behaviour, and it's honestly incredible to watch. You buy official deer crackers from vendors around the park, and the deer have worked out exactly how to earn one: offer them a cracker and they'll bow their heads politely before you feed them. They're scattered through a giant park of shrines, sakura trees and green space, and it's just a really lovely place to spend an afternoon.

One of Nara's sacred deer at golden hour, right outside Todai-ji.

Inside the park is Todai-ji, and I know, we'd seen a lot of temples by now, but this one is a must. It felt like the equivalent of going to see the Taj Mahal. It's one of the largest wooden buildings in the world and home to a bronze Buddha so enormous it's hard to take all of it in as you walk through the door, over a thousand years old and flanked by two other huge statues. Around the side there's a wooden pillar with a hole carved through the base, supposedly the exact size of one of the Buddha's nostrils, and legend says crawling through it guarantees enlightenment in your next life. I'll let you decide whether to chance it.

The Great Buddha of Todai-ji, with one of the gilded statues that flank it.

Kyoto packs a thousand years of history into one city, matcha grown at the source, geisha streets and more temples than you can count, with Nara an easy hop away. You can't do all of it in one trip, and you shouldn't try, but pick a handful off this list and you'll come away feeling like you actually stepped into old Japan.
